Adaptations

One body plan, every habitat

Frogs took a single blueprint — big eyes, loud voice, springy legs — and rebuilt it for canopy, pond, desert, alpine bog and rainforest floor. Eight pieces of equipment that explain how one animal became seven thousand.

Sticky toes

Tree frogs end each toe in a squishy pad covered with microscopic hexagonal cells. A whisper-thin film of mucus between pad and leaf grips by the same physics that keeps a wet glass stuck to a table — so strongly that a frog can hang its whole body from a few toes on a rain-soaked leaf. The pads even self-clean as the frog walks.

Now-you-see-me camouflage

Most frogs dress to disappear. Gray tree frogs re-tint from granite to moss in minutes; peepers pick perches that match their own speckles; leaf-litter toads fade into the forest floor so completely that a walking human finds them only when they jump. And the loud dressers — the poison dart frogs — are advertising on purpose: bright means "don't", and predators learn the lesson once. Red-eyed tree frogs do both, sleeping drab, then flashing hidden scarlet and blue at anything that gets too close.

Poison in the skin

Nearly every frog carries chemical defences in its skin. Toads swell behind the eyes with parotoid glands that ooze bitter toxin; fire-bellied toads flip into a handstand to flash a blazing belly; and dart frogs go further — their batrachotoxins are harvested from the ants and mites they eat, which is why museum-raised ones are harmless. The corroboree frog, alone among vertebrates, cooks its own poisons from scratch.

Antifreeze blood

The wood frog spends winter as a frog-shaped ice cube. Up to two-thirds of its body water freezes solid, its heart stops, and it survives on glucose flooding its cells as cryoprotectant — the same trick as antifreeze. Come spring it thaws, restarts, and is calling within days. It is the only frog found north of the Arctic Circle.

Night-shift eyes

Those enormous golden eyes are low-light lenses: pupils shrink to slits by day and yawn wide at night. Frogs even swallow with them — retracting the eyes into the skull helps press food down the throat. A transparent third eyelid wipes each eye clean underwater.

A built-in megaphone

Male frogs inflate a sac of skin like a balloon and push air through the vocal cords into it, amplifying the call many times over. Each species sings its own tune — a bullfrog's bass "jug-o'-rum", a wood frog's duck-like chuckle, a spring peeper's whistle — and females can pick the right voice out of a chorus of hundreds.

Built-in parachutes

Asia's flying frogs leap from the treetops and simply refuse to fall fast. Enormous webbed hands and feet spread into four living parachutes, skin flaps along the arms add lift, and the glide is steerable — Wallace's flying frog crosses 15 metres of rainforest air, landing toes-first on the next trunk down.

The underground years

Where the world dries out, frogs dig in. Spadefoot toads and the Mexican burrowing toad bury themselves with keratin-hardened heels and wait out the drought; the African bullfrog seals itself into a shed-skin cocoon and can sleep for a year or more. Then the rains come, and a night that was silent becomes a riot of calling — the pond has a deadline, and everyone knows it.

Field notes: home is where the gap is

By day, many frogs simply vanish — into tree hollows, bromeliads, bark flaps and rock crevices, and with zero shame, human hardware. Hideouts like these are as vital as food: keeping a few "messy" corners in a garden can shelter an entire local population.
