Life cycle

Every way to become a frog

The classic story — egg, tadpole, froglet — is only one version. Across seven thousand species, frogs have invented dozens of ways to grow up, from ponds to bromeliads to their own mother's back.

  1. Eggs, wherever they'll be safe

    Most frogs lay jelly-coated eggs in water — but "water" can mean a pond, a stream, a tyre track, a bromeliad funnel, or a foam nest whipped up in a bush. Rainforest species often hang their clutches on leaves above water, and glass frogs and red-eyed tree frogs famously guard them.

  2. Tadpole rain

    Days or weeks later the tadpoles wriggle free and drop, swim or wriggle to their nursery. Red-eyed tree frog embryos can even hatch days early if a snake starts biting into the clutch. Where there is no pond at all, the tadpole grows up in the tiny pool that holds it — a bromeliad, a tree hole, even a nut shell.

  3. The great remodel

    Over the following weeks, legs bud, the tail is absorbed into the body, and lungs take over from gills — one of the most dramatic rebuilds in nature. A few species skip it entirely: their eggs hatch straight into miniature frogs, no pond phase at all.

  4. Out into the world

    A froglet the size of a fingernail climbs out of the water and disperses — up a tree, into a meadow, across a forest floor. A year or three later, on the first warm, wet night of the season, it joins the chorus — and starts the cycle again.

Field notes

Four routes to the same destination

Ask a frog where it spent its childhood and the answer depends wildly on the family.

The classic route

Grow up in the pond

Bullfrogs, green frogs and common toads lay thousands of small eggs in still water and leave them to chance. The tadpoles graze algae for months — bullfrog tadpoles can take two whole years — before the remodel begins.

The aerial route

Grow up over the pond

Tree frogs, glass frogs and flying frogs glue their clutches to leaves above water. The eggs stay warm, oxygenated and out of reach of aquatic predators — and when they hatch, gravity delivers them straight to the nursery.

The carried route

Grow up on a parent

The Surinam toad's eggs sink into the skin of its mother's back, and weeks later fully-formed froglets burst out of her honeycomb of pockets. Darwin's frogs do it in the father's vocal sac; the sadly extinct gastric-brooding frogs did it in the stomach.

The shortcut

Skip the tadpole

Coquí frogs of the Caribbean and many rainforest species lay a handful of large, yolky eggs on land; each hatches as a tiny finished frog. It is the egg-to-frog express — and the reason a coquí can breed in a bromeliad with no pond in sight.
