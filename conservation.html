Conservation

Thin skin, hard century

Frogs breathe and drink through their skin, which makes them the first to feel what happens to a landscape — and roughly four in ten amphibian species are now sliding toward extinction. This is what's pushing them, and what actually helps.

Nowhere left to go

Draining wetlands while clearing forests removes breeding ponds, canopy and hunting ground in a single stroke. Isolated populations then wink out quietly, pond by pond, valley by valley.

A fungus among frogs

Chytridiomycosis, caused by the fungus Bd, has hit amphibians on every continent — implicated in the decline of hundreds of species and the outright loss of dozens. It thrives in cool mountain streams, right where so many frogs breed.

Poisons on the ground

Pesticides, herbicides and road run-off wash into breeding ponds. A frog's permeable skin absorbs it all; populations thin out long before anyone thinks to count them.

Stowaways and releases

Invasive species — cane toads, Cuban tree frogs, released pets, farmed bullfrogs shipped worldwide — out-eat and out-breed natives, and ship Bd and other diseases with them.

A shifting calendar

Climate change scrambles the timing frogs live by: breeding ponds dry before tadpoles finish, alpine snowpack shrinks, and warm nights cue choruses at the wrong moment. A pond with a deadline is a dangerous nursery.

The harvest

Hundreds of millions of frogs are caught or farmed each year for frog legs and the pet trade. Some wild populations are collected faster than they can replace themselves.

Spotlight

Rabb's tree frog Ecnomiohyla rabborum

A canopy giant of Panama's cloud forests that could glide tree-to-tree on webbed hands and feet. Its story ended in 2016, when "Toughie", the last known member of the species, died in captivity. Habitat loss and chytrid were the closing chapters — one of some two hundred amphibian species lost in recent decades, and a reminder of what the other six thousand are worth.

What actually helps

  • Keep — or dig — a fish-free garden pond. Even a barrel pond will do.
  • Skip the pesticides. Let the bugs live so the frogs can eat.
  • Drive slowly on warm, rainy spring nights — that's amphibian rush hour.
  • Join a frog survey or submit sightings to iNaturalist — counts steer protection.
  • Never release or relocate a frog, and choose captive-bred pets. Diseases travel with animals.
  • Support forest and wetland protection, at home and in the tropics.
