The family tree

Fourteen families, one order

More than sixty families of frogs exist; these are the branches most likely to cross your path — each photographed through a wild family member. Click any photo for a closer look.

True tree frogs

Hylidae

Where
Americas, Europe, Asia
Species
1,000+

The archetypal climbing frogs: sticky toe pads, slim waists and forward eyes. This one family spans the spring peeper of Canadian ponds and the giant Cuban tree frog — and gave this guide its start.

  • Toe pads
  • Champions of chorus

True frogs

Ranidae

Where
Worldwide except Australasia
Species
~500

The pond-and-stream specialists: long, powerful hind legs and webbed feet. The American bullfrog is the family's giant — a mouth that opens wide enough to swallow other frogs, birds and snakes.

  • Strong jumpers
  • Pond dwellers

True toads

Bufonidae

Where
Every continent except Antarctica and Australia
Species
~600

Dry, warty, poison-glanded walkers with a taste for gardens and night walks. Behind each eye sits a parotoid gland that makes most predators regret their choice — and makes toads among the most successful land frogs.

  • Parotoid glands
  • Eggs in strings

Poison dart frogs

Dendrobatidae

Where
Central & South America
Species
~200

Thumb-sized jewels that advertise their toxins in daylight colour. Their poisons come from the ants and mites they eat — and some species carry their tadpoles uphill, one by one, to private water pools.

  • Toxic skin
  • Doting parents

Glass frogs

Centrolenidae

Where
Neotropical cloud forests
Species
~160

Skin so clear you can watch the heart beat and the gut work. They sit on leaves over streams by night; their eggs hatch into tadpoles that drop straight into the current below.

  • Transparent skin
  • Leaf-sitters

Eurasian tree frogs

Rhacophoridae

Where
Africa & tropical Asia
Species
~450

Asia and Africa's answer to the hylids — plus the "flying frogs," which parachute between trees on oversized webbed feet, and the foam-nest builders whose eggs hatch inside whipped-up froth hung over water.

  • Gliders
  • Foam nests

Narrow-mouthed frogs

Microhylidae

Where
Worldwide in the tropics
Species
~700

A grab-bag family of tiny, pointy-headed frogs with narrow mouths made for ants and termites. Madagascar's tomato frog shows the family's louder side: a round, red, glue-oozing advertisement that it is a terrible meal.

  • Ant specialists
  • Hugely varied

Clawed frogs & Surinam toads

Pipidae

Where
South America & Africa
Species
~40

Fully aquatic relics that never leave the water — tongueless, claw-toed, sensing prey by water currents. Their tadpoles swim in schools like fish, and the Surinam toad carries its eggs embedded in the skin of its back.

  • Fully aquatic
  • No tongue
  • Ancient lineage

Australian ground frogs

Myobatrachidae

Where
Australia & New Guinea
Species
~130

Australia's home-grown oddballs: frogs that swallow their eggs and give birth through the mouth, gastric-brooding oddities, and the corroboree frogs — snowline alpine frogs that make their own poison from scratch.

  • Self-made toxins
  • Alpine survivors

Horned frogs

Ceratophryidae

Where
South America
Species
~12

Half mouth, half ambush: the Pac-Man frogs of the pet trade. A horned frog buries itself in leaf litter with only its eyelid "horns" showing, then detonates upward at anything edible — including frogs nearly its own size.

  • Ambush hunters
  • Bottomless appetite

Fire-bellied toads

Bombinatoridae

Where
Europe & Asia
Species
~15

Small pond frogs that dare predators to try: when grabbed, they arch back and flash a belly of warning colours while secreting milky skin toxins. Their mournful, hooting "unk" calls drift over Asian wetlands each spring.

  • Unken reflex
  • Glowing bellies

African bullfrogs

Pyxicephalidae

Where
Sub-Saharan Africa
Species
~85

Frogs with the temperament of small crocodiles. The giant African bullfrog eats scorpions, other frogs and small mammals; males guard their tadpole pools through drought, digging channels to rescue stranded young.

  • Fierce fathers
  • Up to 2 kg

Burrowing toad

Rhinophrynidae

Where
Central America & Texas
Species
1 — the only one

The lone survivor of a 190-million-year-old lineage: a globe-shaped digging machine with a tongue modified to scoop termites underground. It spends most of the year buried, emerging only for explosive breeding floods.

  • Living fossil
  • Termite vacuum

Australasian tree frogs

Pelodryadidae

Where
Australia & New Guinea
Species
~220

Australia's own tree-frog lineage, long treated as hylid cousins and now recognised as their own ancient family — from the laid-back White's tree frog to torrent-dwelling rock frogs with tadpoles that grip waterfalls.

  • Gondwanan lineage
  • Iconic locals

And the tree keeps branching: the Neotropical Leptodactylidae, the Madagascar mantellids, the Indian Nasikabatrachus "purple frog" that surfaces two days a year — more than sixty families in all, with new branches still being discovered.
