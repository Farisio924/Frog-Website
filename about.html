About

What is a frog?

Nearly 8,000 species, one body plan: no tail, legs built like springs, and a life that begins in water. Everything else is improvisation.

Frogs and toads together form the order Anura — Greek for "without a tail" — and they make up roughly nine of every ten amphibian species on Earth. The recipe is ancient and reliable: a short, stiff spine that acts like a launch frame; hind legs loaded with muscle and tendon so the whole animal can fire itself forward like a sprung trap; a wide mouth and sticky tongue for grabbing prey whole; and skin that breathes, drinks and sometimes poisons.

From that one chassis, frogs have colonised an astonishing range of lives. There are frogs that live their whole lives in ponds, frogs that never meet a pond at all, frogs that glide between trees, frogs that burrow like moles, frogs that survive being frozen solid, and frogs so toxic a single skin touch was once used to make hunting darts lethal. They sit at the hinge of water and land, and their permeable skin makes them among the first animals to register a sickening wetland — one reason frogs are called environmental sentinels.

Almost everything a frog does, it does with drama: eyes that retract to help swallow, eardrums you can see from across the room, and breeding choruses loud enough to drown out traffic. The rest of this guide is a tour through that variety — family by family, species by species.

The oldest question in the pond

Frog or toad?

"Toad" is not a scientific category — it's a look. Some of the wariest, driest-skinned "toads" are more closely related to tree frogs than to other toads. The labels are folklore that happens to be useful.

The classic "frog"

Sleek and wet

Smooth, moist skin; long legs for leaping and swimming; teeth in the upper jaw; eggs in loose clusters. Think green frogs and bullfrogs — the pond-side archetype.

The classic "toad"

Warty and dry

Rough, waterproof skin; parotoid glands behind the eyes that secrete toxins; short legs built for walking; eggs laid in long strings. Common toads and cane toads set the stereotype.

The rule-breakers

Everything in between

Fire-bellied toads flash poison from wet skin; horned frogs are frogs with a toad's appetite; Surinam toads look like leaves and never leave the water. The tree of "frog vs toad" is thoroughly scrambled.

A useful shortcut anyway

Why it still works

What folk wisdom calls a "toad" is usually a land-living, burrowing frog with poison glands — a real ecological role, just not a single family. Call it a toad and no biologist will mind; they'll just smile.
