Species guide

Twenty-four real characters

All photographed alive and in the wild — from a see-through leaf-sitter in Costa Rica to a frozen-solid northerner, a back-brooding leaf and the most poisonous animal on Earth. Click any photo for a closer look.

Chapter one

Tree frogs & canopy acrobats

The leaf-top specialists: sticky pads, night eyes and voices built for the dark. This is where the guide began.

Red-eyed tree frog

Agalychnis callidryas

Range
Southern Mexico to Colombia
Size
4–7 cm

Sleeps flattened against a leaf by day. When startled, it flashes scarlet eyes, orange feet and blue-and-yellow flanks — a split-second "startle show" that buys time to leap. Females glue egg clutches to leaves overhanging ponds, and the embryos can hatch days early to escape attacking snakes.

  • Startle colours
  • Rainforest night
  • Eggs on leaves

American green tree frog

Hyla cinerea

Range
Southeastern United States
Size
3–6 cm

The backyard classic: on summer evenings it clings to window screens and porch lights, picking off the insects drawn to the glow. Its nasal, cowbell-like "queenk" is the soundtrack of southern ponds — and it is official enough to be Louisiana's state amphibian.

  • Backyard neighbour
  • State amphibian

White's tree frog

Litoria caerulea

Range
Australia & New Guinea
Size
7–11 cm

Plump, sleepy-eyed and famously easygoing — the "dumpy frog" can live twenty years or more in captivity. In the wild it crowds into tree hollows, pipe openings and even toilet cisterns, and its skin secretions are studied for antibacterial compounds.

  • Long-lived
  • Comfort-loving

European tree frog

Hyla arborea

Range
Europe into western Asia
Size
3–5 cm

Thumb-sized and bright as a fresh leaf, it sunbathes on reeds by day and sings all night — a male's rattling call carries hundreds of metres from a frog lighter than a coin. Each winter it tucks into leaf litter or a wall crevice and waits for spring.

  • Tiny but loud
  • Reed acrobat

Gray tree frog

Hyla versicolor

Range
Eastern North America
Size
3–6 cm

A living colour-chart: it fades between mottled gray and leafy green in minutes to match its perch. Come winter it floods its body with glucose antifreeze, lets most of it freeze solid, and restarts its heart in spring. It's a near-perfect twin of Cope's gray tree frog — experts tell them apart mostly by voice.

  • Freeze-tolerant
  • Colour-changer

Valerio's glass frog

Hyalinobatrachium valerioi

Range
Costa Rica & Panama
Size
~2.5 cm

Its underside is genuine window glass: heart, liver and gut sit in plain view, and the "ghost" outline confuses predators hunting from below. Males stay beside the egg clutches they fertilise, guarding them night after night against wasps and snakes.

  • See-through skin
  • Devoted dad

Spring peeper

Pseudacris crucifer

Range
Eastern North America
Size
2–3.5 cm

A frog the mass of a couple of coins, with a voice like sleigh bells — on big-chorus nights, thousands of peepers can be heard well over a kilometre away. Look for the dark cross on its back that gives the species its name: crucifer, the cross-bearer.

  • First call of spring
  • Micro songster

Cuban tree frog

Osteopilus septentrionalis

Range
Cuba & Bahamas; invasive in Florida
Size
up to ~14 cm

Hitched a ride to Florida in shipping crates about a century ago and stayed: it is now North America's largest tree frog, happily eating its native cousins and setting up house in toilets and drainpipes. Its sticky skin secretion can irritate your eyes — one more reason to never relocate amphibians.

  • Invasive giant
  • Tree-hole dweller

Chapter two

Pond frogs & the fully aquatic

The classic water frogs — plus two tongueless oddballs that live out their whole lives underwater.

American bullfrog

Lithobates catesbeianus

Range
Native eastern North America; introduced worldwide
Size
up to 20 cm

North America's largest frog, named for its booming "jug-o-rum" call. Nothing that fits in its enormous mouth is safe — insects, fish, other frogs, snakes, even bats snatched mid-air. Farmed for food, it has hopped into ecosystems on four continents, where it eats its way through local wildlife.

  • Booming bass voice
  • Invasive abroad

Wood frog

Lithobates sylvaticus

Range
Alaska to the Appalachians
Size
3.5–8 cm

The only frog found north of the Arctic Circle — because it simply freezes each winter. Its cells load with glucose as antifreeze, its heart stops, up to two-thirds of its body water turns to ice, and in spring it thaws and hops off. Males call in duck-like chuckles the moment the melt ponds open.

  • Freezes solid
  • Arctic circle

African clawed frog

Xenopus laevis

Range
Sub-Saharan Africa; invasive on five continents
Size
~10 cm

A tongueless, fully aquatic frog that hunts by feeling water currents with a fish-like lateral line, and gets its name from the black claws on its hind toes. For decades it was the world's pregnancy test — a woman's urine made it lay eggs — and lab escapees have colonised ponds from California to Wales.

  • Never leaves water
  • Medical pioneer

Surinam toad

Pipa pipa

Range
Amazon basin
Size
10–17 cm

A dead-leaf lookalike that never leaves the water. Mating pairs somersault through looping arcs in mid-water; at the top of each arc, eggs are pressed into the female's back, where her skin swells around them. Weeks later, fully-formed froglets erupt from the honeycomb — one of the strangest births in nature.

  • Back brooding
  • Leaf mimic

Chapter three

Toads & burrowers

Dry-skinned, poison-glanded, built for walking and digging — the night shift of the frog world.

Common toad

Bufo bufo

Range
Europe into western Asia
Size
6–13 cm

Europe's garden classic: it walks rather than hops, spends daylight buried in soil or under pots, and hoovers up slugs by torchlight. Each spring it walks kilometres back to the pond where it was born — so faithfully that volunteers run "toad crossings" to ferry them over busy roads.

  • Garden resident
  • Pond pilgrim

Cane toad

Rhinella marina

Range
Native Central & South America; introduced across the tropics
Size
10–24 cm

A toad the size of a dinner plate carrying poison glands to match. Introduced to Australia in 1935 to eat cane beetles, it instead began one of ecology's most famous invasions — advancing west ever since, poisoning predators naive to its toxins, and evolving longer legs as it goes.

  • Toxic invader
  • Dinner-plate size

Corroboree frog

Pseudophryne corroboree

Range
Australian Alps, above 1,300 m
Size
2.5–3 cm

A thumb-sized frog in harlequin yellow and black that lives where frogs shouldn't — in snowy alpine bogs — and synthesises its own unique poisons from scratch, something almost no other frog does. Its eggs overwinter on land, waiting for flood, and its entire wild population now numbers in the low hundreds.

  • Self-made poison
  • Critically endangered

Mexican burrowing toad

Rhinophrynus dorsalis

Range
Texas to Honduras
Size
6–8 cm

The only living species of a family 190 million years old — a genuine living fossil. Spherical, spade-footed and orange-striped, it spends the dry season sealed underground and surfaces only during monsoon floods, when males inflate and let out a call like an air-raid siren.

  • Living fossil
  • Siren call

African bullfrog

Pyxicephalus adspersus

Range
Sub-Saharan Africa
Size
up to 25 cm, ~2 kg

A frog with the temperament of a small crocodile: it eats scorpions, rodents and other frogs, and breeding males brawl like sumo wrestlers over pond territory. Those same males then guard their tadpole pools for weeks — even digging escape channels to stranded young as the water dries.

  • Fearsome father
  • Cocoon sleeper

Chapter four

Poison dart frogs

Jewel-bright, daylight-bold, and toxic in proportion to their diet of ants and mites.

Golden poison frog

Phyllobates terribilis

Range
Pacific rainforests of Colombia
Size
up to ~4.7 cm

Widely considered the most poisonous vertebrate on Earth: a single wild frog carries enough batrachotoxin to kill several adults, harvested from the beetles and ants it eats. Colombia's Emberá people used it to poison blow-dart tips — hence the family name. It advertises all of this in pure, unmissable yellow.

  • Deadliest vertebrate
  • Dart poison source

Dyeing poison dart frog

Dendrobates tinctorius

Range
Guiana Shield, South America
Size
up to ~5 cm

One species wearing a dozen costumes: from yellow-striped giants to the famous all-blue "azureus" morph once classed as its own species. The strange name comes from indigenous peoples reportedly using its toxins to turn captive parrots' feathers new colours.

  • Dozens of morphs
  • Toxic diet

Strawberry poison dart frog

Oophaga pumilio

Range
Caribbean lowlands, Nicaragua to Panama
Size
~2 cm

The "blue jeans" frog: a red body, denim-blue legs, and a parenting routine worthy of a documentary. A mother carries each tadpole up a tree to its own bromeliad pool, then returns every few days to feed it unfertilised eggs — a private education paid in caviar.

  • Egg-feeder mother
  • Blue jeans

Chapter five

Oddballs & record holders

Flash-marked bellies, mouths like traps, feet like parachutes and glue like bubblegum — the family's medal table.

Fire-bellied toad

Bombina orientalis

Range
Korea, northeastern China, far-eastern Russia
Size
4–5.5 cm

A pond frog wearing warning colours under its coat. When grabbed, it arches into the "unken reflex" — head and legs up, blazing belly out — while its skin weeps milky toxins. On spring evenings the males float and hoot a soft, descending "unk… unk" that carries across the reeds.

  • Unken reflex
  • Hooting call

Amazon horned frog

Ceratophrys cornuta

Range
Amazon basin
Size
7–12 cm

Half mouth, half patience. This ambush specialist buries itself in leaf litter with only its eyelids — extended into soft "horns" that break up its outline — above ground, then explodes upward at anything that moves. Its bite can subdue prey nearly its own size, including other horned frogs.

  • Ambush mouth
  • Eyelid horns

Wallace's flying frog

Rhacophorus nigropalmatus

Range
Borneo, Sumatra, Malay Peninsula
Size
~10 cm

Named for Alfred Russel Wallace, co-discoverer of natural selection, who sent the first specimen to London. Between rainforest trees it launches into a controlled glide of 15 metres or more, spreading enormous webbed hands and feet like four black parachutes before landing toes-first on a trunk.

  • Glides 15 m+
  • Wallace's specimen

Tomato frog

Dyscophus antongilii

Range
Northeastern Madagascar
Size
6–10 cm

Madagascar's ripe-fruit impersonator. Big females glow tomato-red; a threatened tomato frog inflates and secretes a sticky white gloop that gums shut the mouth and eyes of whatever bit it — glue rather than poison. Confined to a small corner of one island, it is among the world's more vulnerable frogs.

  • Glue defence
  • Madagascar endemic
